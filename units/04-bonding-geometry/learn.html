Bonding, Nomenclature & Geometry

How do bonds and molecular shape explain material properties?

A formula tells you which atoms are present, but it does not tell the whole story. The way electrons hold particles together, the three-dimensional arrangement of atoms, and the attractions between particles all help determine a substance’s properties.

01 · Distinguish ionic, covalent, and metallic bonding and use electronegativity as evidence.

Three models of bonding

In simpler language

Ions attract, covalent bonds share electron density, and metals have mobile electrons. Unequal sharing makes a bond polar.

An ionic bond is attraction between oppositely charged ions in an extended structure. Electron transfer explains ion formation; attraction is the bond itself. Covalent bonding involves shared electron density. Metallic bonding involves mobile, delocalized electrons attracting metal centers.

The electronegativity difference suggests how unevenly electrons are shared:

Δχ = |χ₁ − χ₂|

Small differences often indicate nearly nonpolar bonds; intermediate differences, polar covalent bonds; large differences, substantial ionic character. These lie on a continuum. Mission cutoffs are classroom conventions; also consider element types and actual structure.

In polar bonds, δ+ and δ− mean partial charges, not full ion charges. Breaking bonds requires energy; forming bonds releases it. Net reaction energy depends on all changed bonds and interactions.

Think of it this way. A shared blanket pulled unevenly can suggest unequal electron sharing. Unlike people, atoms do not want, choose, or deliberately steal electrons.

Worked example

For H and Cl, use electronegativities 2.20 and 3.16:

Δχ = |2.20 − 3.16| = 0.96

The bond is polar covalent; chlorine is partially negative. An isolated HCl molecule is not a pair of fully separated ions.

02 · Write and name common ionic and molecular compounds, including variable-charge metals and polyatomic ions.

Names and formulas must balance charge

In simpler language

For ions, balance charges. For binary molecules, read the prefixes as atom counts.

An ionic formula is electrically neutral. Choose the smallest whole-number ratio of ions that makes total charge zero. Name the cation first, then a monatomic anion with an -ide ending. A Roman numeral specifies the charge of a variable-charge metal: iron(III) means Fe³⁺, not three iron atoms. Keep a polyatomic ion together; use parentheses when more than one is required.

For binary molecular compounds, prefixes count atoms: mono-, di-, tri-, tetra-, penta-, hexa-, hepta-, octa-, nona-, and deca-. Usually omit mono- from the first element, and give the second an -ide ending. Carbon monoxide and carbon dioxide have different formulas and properties. Do not apply the ionic charge-balancing shortcut to covalent names that already specify atom counts.

Use these common ion references when building formulas: Group 1 cations +1; Group 2 +2; Al³⁺; Zn²⁺; Ag⁺; fluoride F⁻, chloride Cl⁻, bromide Br⁻, oxide O²⁻, and sulfide S²⁻. Common polyatomic ions are ammonium NH₄⁺, hydroxide OH⁻, nitrate NO₃⁻, carbonate CO₃²⁻, sulfate SO₄²⁻, and phosphate PO₄³⁻. Unlike a monatomic anion, a polyatomic ion keeps its established name. These references and a Roman numeral let you determine the ratio without guessing.

Think of it this way. A balanced account can contain different numbers of credits and debits as long as their total values cancel. Ion ratios similarly depend on charge magnitude, not equal particle counts.

Worked example

Al³⁺ and O²⁻ form Al₂O₃: +6 and −6 cancel. Ca²⁺ with nitrate, NO₃⁻, forms Ca(NO₃)₂. FeCl₃ is iron(III) chloride. N₂O₄ is dinitrogen tetroxide. Parentheses multiply the entire ion, not only its final atom.

03 · Count electron domains and distinguish electron-domain arrangement from molecular geometry.

Lewis structures lead to three-dimensional shape

In simpler language

Count bonds and lone pairs around the center, then name the arrangement of the atoms.

For a simple Lewis structure: count valence electrons, select a reasonable center, add single bonds, fill terminal shells then the center, and add multiple bonds if needed. H needs two electrons; many main-group atoms favor eight. BF₃ is a valid octet exception.

VSEPR minimizes domain repulsion. Each bond (single, double, or triple) and each lone pair counts as one domain:

2 domains: linear, 180°
3 domains: trigonal planar, 120°
4 domains: tetrahedral, about 109.5°

Molecular geometry counts atom positions, omitting lone pairs. Four domains give tetrahedral CH₄ (no lone pairs), trigonal pyramidal NH₃ (one; ≈107°), or bent H₂O (two; ≈104.5°). CO₂ has two bonding domains and is linear.

SO₂ is bent with three domains (two bonds, one lone pair; angle near 120°). Attached-atom count alone cannot determine shape. Linear BeF₂ here is an isolated gas-phase molecule; its solid is an extended structure.

Think of it this way. Balloons tied at a point spread apart, suggesting domain repulsion. Electron distributions are quantum objects, not inflated balls.

Worked example

Water has eight valence electrons:

6 from O + 2 from H = 8 electrons

Two O–H bonds use four electrons. Four remain as two lone pairs. Four domains give a tetrahedral electron arrangement but a bent molecular shape.

TEKS C.7(D) analyze the properties of ionic, covalent, and metallic substances in terms of intramolecular and intermolecular forces.

04 · Relate particle mobility and attractions to conductivity, phase changes, and physical properties.

From particles to material properties

In simpler language

The kind of particles, how strongly they attract, and whether charge can move help explain a material’s behavior.

Ionic solids are often hard, brittle, and high-melting. Their ions cannot freely carry current in a fixed lattice; when melted or dissolved, mobile ions can conduct. Metals conduct through mobile electrons and are often malleable because layers can shift while metallic attraction persists. Many molecular substances have lower melting or boiling points, but network covalent solids, such as diamond, are major exceptions.

Intermolecular attractions act between molecules; covalent bonds act within them. Melting or boiling a molecular substance usually changes intermolecular organization without breaking molecules into atoms. All atoms and molecules exhibit London dispersion forces. Polar molecules also have dipole–dipole attractions. Hydrogen bonding is especially important when H is bonded to N, O, or F and interacts with a suitable lone pair nearby.

Water’s polar, bent molecules form an open hydrogen-bonded structure in ordinary ice. The same mass occupies more volume than in liquid water, so ice is less dense and floats. Floating ice can insulate water beneath. Lake freezing also depends on depth, weather, and heat transfer; molecular geometry alone does not guarantee that every lake stays unfrozen.

Think of it this way. Loose people can walk through a room, while people fixed to assigned seats cannot carry messages by moving around. This helps distinguish mobile charge carriers from fixed ions.

Worked example

A solid salt that does not conduct can conduct after dissolving if it releases mobile ions. Dissolved sugar generally does not conduct appreciably because its molecules remain uncharged. This comparison requires the same testing conditions.

Honors A continuum, not a switch

Honors extension · builds on these expectations

TEKS C.7(A) construct an argument to support how periodic trends such as electronegativity can predict bonding between elements;

The mission’s Pauling estimate is

percent ionic character = 100[1 − exp(−0.25(Δχ)²)]

At Δχ = 1.0, the estimate is about 22%. It is an empirical model of bond character, not the percentage of molecules that have become ions.

Challenge: Does the estimate mean 22 of every 100 bonds are purely ionic?

Show the reasoning

No. It assigns a continuous character to a bond. It is not a count of two separate populations.

A polar bond does not automatically make a polar molecule. Bond dipoles have directions. In linear CO₂ the equivalent C–O bond dipoles cancel; in bent H₂O they do not. Symmetric BF₃ is nonpolar overall, while trigonal pyramidal NH₃ is polar.

Challenge: Why can a formula with polar bonds still describe a nonpolar molecule?

Show the reasoning

Its geometry may make the dipoles cancel. Both bond polarity and spatial arrangement are required.

Honors Choose the dominant intermolecular attraction

Honors extension · builds on these expectations

TEKS C.7(D) analyze the properties of ionic, covalent, and metallic substances in terms of intramolecular and intermolecular forces.

Identify whether hydrogen bonding is possible; if not, examine permanent dipoles and dispersion. All molecules have dispersion, and large, easily polarized molecules can have strong dispersion. Do not assume a small polar molecule always has a higher boiling point than a much larger nonpolar one.

Challenge: Compare similar-sized CH₄, HCl, and H₂O. Which special attraction is available only to water?

Show the reasoning

Water can form strong O–H···O hydrogen bonds. CH₄ relies on dispersion; HCl has dipole–dipole attraction as well as dispersion.

Keep the language precise

Glossary

Ionic bond
Electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions.
Covalent bond
Attraction involving shared electron density between atoms.
Metallic bond
Attraction involving metal centers and delocalized electrons.
Polyatomic ion
A bonded group of atoms with a net charge.
Lewis structure
A diagram of bonding and nonbonding valence electrons.
Electron domain
One region of electron density around a central atom.
Lone pair
A pair of valence electrons not used in a bond.
VSEPR
A model that predicts geometry from electron-domain repulsion.
Dipole
A separation of positive and negative charge.
Dispersion force
Attraction associated with fluctuating electron distributions.
Hydrogen bond
An attraction involving an N–H, O–H, or F–H group and a suitable acceptor.
