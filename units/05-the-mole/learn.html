The Mole & Chemical Quantities

How can a balance count particles too small to see?

Chemists handle visible samples but explain them using atoms, molecules, and ions. The mole connects those scales. You will use mass to count particles, analyze composition, and infer formulas before applying those tools to a life-support case.

01 · Convert between moles and specified particles using Avogadro’s constant.

A counting unit on an enormous scale

In simpler language

A mole is a counting package. Say exactly what is being counted.

One mole contains exactly 6.02214076 × 10²³ specified entities: atoms, molecules, ions, or formula units. Always name the entity. The Avogadro constant NA is this count per mole:

Moles → particles
N = nNA
Particles → moles
n = N/NA

A mole is neither a fixed mass nor a fixed volume. One mole of He atoms and one mole of water molecules have equal entity counts but different masses. Each H₂O molecule has two H atoms and one O atom; therefore one mole of H₂O contains two moles of H atoms and one mole of O atoms. Ionic NaCl is counted in formula units, not separate molecules.

Think of it this way. A dozen eggs and a dozen bowling balls have the same count but different masses. A mole uses a much larger fixed count.

Worked example

For 0.250 mol CO₂:

N = 0.250 × 6.022 × 10²³
N = 1.51 × 10²³ CO₂ molecules

Two O atoms per molecule give:

N(O) = 2N(CO₂) = 3.01 × 10²³ O atoms

02 · Calculate molar mass and convert between mass, amount, particles, and a stated gas volume.

The bridge between grams and moles

In simpler language

Divide by molar mass to go from grams to moles. Multiply to return to grams.

Molar mass M is mass per mole, usually in g/mol. Add each element’s atomic mass multiplied by its formula subscript. Parentheses multiply every atom inside them.

Grams → moles
n = m/M
Moles → grams
m = nM

Check the units: g divided by g/mol leaves mol.

The mole is the central conversion point. To move from mass to particles, first convert grams to moles and then multiply by NA. Reverse those steps to find mass from a particle count. Predict the scale first: a mass smaller than one molar mass represents less than one mole.

The missions may use the approximate molar gas volume 22.4 L/mol at 0 °C and 1 atm for an ideal gas. This is a stated condition, not a universal value. At other temperatures or pressures, use the gas-law relationship developed in Unit 7. Liquids and solids do not obey the 22.4 L/mol shortcut.

Think of it this way. If a package has a known mass per dozen identical items, its total mass tells you how many dozens it contains. Molar mass provides the same kind of conversion for a specified chemical substance.

Worked example

For water:

M ≈ 2(1.008) + 16.00 = 18.02 g/mol
n = 9.01 g / 18.02 g/mol = 0.500 mol
N ≈ 0.500 × 6.022 × 10²³ = 3.01 × 10²³ molecules

At 0 °C and 1 atm, for an ideal gas:

V ≈ (0.500 mol)(22.4 L/mol) = 11.2 L

03 · Calculate percent composition and compare a measured composition with a specification.

Composition is a mass fraction

In simpler language

Find the mass of the part, divide by the mass of the whole, and multiply by 100.

Percent composition by mass compares one element’s mass with the compound’s total mass:

Mass percent = (element mass / compound mass) × 100%

Use a one-mole formula calculation: find each element’s mass contribution and divide by molar mass. Percentages sum to about 100%, allowing for rounding.

Mass percentage is not atom percentage. Oxygen supplies most of water’s mass even though H atoms outnumber O atoms two to one. For the purity mission, calculate the expected percentage, then compare the reported value and allowed tolerance. One matching percentage cannot alone prove real-sample purity or identity; the mission uses a simplified evidence rule.

Think of it this way. A small number of heavy textbooks can make up most of a backpack’s mass even if pencils outnumber them. Counts and mass fractions answer different questions.

Worked example

Oxygen’s mass fraction in water is:

% O = (16.00/18.02) × 100% ≈ 88.79%

A ±1.5 percentage-point tolerance is an absolute difference between percentage values, not a relative 1.5% change.

TEKS C.8(D) differentiate between empirical and molecular formulas.

04 · Derive an empirical ratio and use molar mass to determine a molecular formula.

From composition to empirical and molecular formulas

In simpler language

Convert masses to mole ratios first. The empirical formula is the simplest ratio; the molecular formula is a whole-number multiple.

An empirical formula gives the smallest whole-number atom ratio; a molecular formula gives the actual count per molecule. Glucose C₆H₁₂O₆ has empirical formula CH₂O. Ionic formulas ordinarily give the simplest charge-balanced ratio.

Convert each element’s mass to moles, divide all amounts by the smallest, then find whole-number ratios. For percentage data, assume a 100 g sample. Do not round 1.5 to 2: multiply every ratio by 2. Ratios near 1.33 or 1.67 may require multiplying all by 3; respect data precision.

A known molecular molar mass gives the subscript multiplier:

k = molecular molar mass / empirical-formula mass

Multiply every empirical subscript by the integer k. In the capstone, establish identity and then apply the composition tolerance to the approve, quarantine, or reject decision.

Think of it this way. A tile pattern of one red, two white, and one blue tile can repeat several times. The simplest pattern resembles an empirical formula; the total tile count resembles a molecular formula.

Worked example

Assume 100 g for 40.0% C, 6.7% H, 53.3% O:

n(C) ≈ 3.33; n(H) ≈ 6.65; n(O) ≈ 3.33 mol
C : H : O ≈ 1 : 2 : 1 → CH₂O

With molecular molar mass about 180 g/mol:

k ≈ 180/30.03 ≈ 6 → C₆H₁₂O₆

05 · Use a supplied balanced equation to connect amounts of two substances.

Read a reaction as a mole recipe

In simpler language

Use the equation’s coefficient ratio between moles, then convert to grams if needed.

Balanced coefficients compare particles and moles, not grams. Put the desired substance in the numerator of a mole-ratio conversion factor:

2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O

Two moles of H₂ react with one mole of O₂ to form two moles of H₂O.

For the Apollo 13 scrubber model:

2LiOH + CO₂ → Li₂CO₃ + H₂O

Each mole of CO₂ requires two moles of LiOH. Convert CO₂ amount to moles of LiOH, then to mass. Real canister performance also depends on airflow and design; this equation specifies chemical proportions, not complete engineering performance.

Think of it this way. A recipe requiring two bread slices per sandwich scales by a ratio. Chemical coefficients similarly scale quantities while preserving the reaction’s proportions.

Worked example

For 5.00 mol CO₂, use the 2:1 mole ratio:

n(LiOH) = 5.00 mol CO₂ × (2 mol LiOH / 1 mol CO₂)
n(LiOH) = 10.0 mol
m(LiOH) = 10.0 × 23.95 ≈ 2.40 × 10² g

This is a capacity calculation under the stated reaction model.

Honors Count water in a crystal

Honors extension · builds on these expectations

TEKS C.8(D) differentiate between empirical and molecular formulas.

A hydrate contains water in a defined ratio, salt·xH₂O. Find water lost from initial mass minus dry-salt mass, then convert both masses to moles. For this example:

n(CuSO₄) = 1.60/159.61 ≈ 0.0100 mol
n(H₂O) = 0.90/18.02 ≈ 0.0499 mol
x = n(H₂O)/n(CuSO₄) ≈ 5

Incomplete drying makes the dry mass too large and water loss too small, lowering inferred x. Decomposition or spattering causes other errors.

Challenge: Why might incomplete drying give the wrong x?

Show the reasoning

The apparent dry mass would be too large and the water loss too small, lowering the inferred hydration number. Decomposition or spattering would introduce different errors.

Honors Reconstruct a formula from combustion products

Honors extension · builds on these expectations

TEKS C.8(C) calculate percent composition of compounds; and

TEKS C.8(D) differentiate between empirical and molecular formulas.

For complete combustion of a sample containing only C, H, and O, the products reveal carbon and hydrogen amounts:

n(C) = n(CO₂)
n(H) = 2n(H₂O)

Convert those amounts to masses. Obtain sample oxygen by subtraction:

m(O) = m(sample) − m(C) − m(H)

Then convert oxygen mass to moles and find the smallest atom ratio. Subtraction is invalid if other elements are present: product oxygen also includes oxygen supplied during combustion.

Challenge: A product sample contains 0.020 mol CO₂ and 0.030 mol H₂O. What C:H atom ratio follows?

Show the reasoning
n(C) = 0.020 mol
n(H) = 2 × 0.030 = 0.060 mol
C:H = 1:3

Sample oxygen requires sample mass minus C/H masses. Product oxygen also comes from supplied combustion gas.

Keep the language precise

Glossary

Mole
An amount containing exactly 6.02214076 × 10²³ specified entities.
Avogadro constant
The number of specified entities per mole.
Molar mass
The mass of one mole of a substance.
Formula unit
The ratio unit used to describe an ionic compound.
Percent composition
An element’s mass fraction expressed as a percentage.
Empirical formula
The simplest whole-number atom ratio.
Molecular formula
The actual atom counts in a molecule.
Mole ratio
A ratio of amounts supplied by a balanced equation.
Hydrate
A crystalline compound containing water in a characteristic ratio.
Combustion analysis
Inferring composition from measured combustion products.
