Practices, Measurement & Matter

How can a measurement become trustworthy evidence?

A chemistry investigation begins before a calculation. You must decide what you are observing, select a suitable tool, record its units, and judge how much confidence the result deserves. These habits connect a small aquarium investigation to the navigation of a spacecraft.

Read and explore first. Use the practice checks to find ideas to revisit. Then demonstrate your understanding in the missions and apply it in the case file.

01 · Distinguish substances, mixtures, and physical or chemical changes; separate an observation from an explanation.

Matter, models, and evidence

In simpler language

First describe what happened. Then use evidence to explain why it happened.

Matter has mass and occupies space. An element contains one kind of atom. A compound contains elements chemically combined in a fixed ratio. A mixture contains substances in variable proportions: salt water is homogeneous at the scale we observe, whereas sand in water is heterogeneous. Filtration separates suspended solids; evaporation can recover a dissolved solid. These methods use physical properties.

A physical change preserves chemical identity, as when ice melts. A chemical change produces different substances. Bubbles, color changes, or temperature changes can be evidence, but none alone proves a reaction: boiling water also bubbles. Record what you see before explaining it. A useful investigation changes one relevant variable while controlling others, repeats measurements, and compares the result with a reference. Follow the teacher’s procedure and use the specified protective equipment; the aquarium missions are classroom models, not instructions for treating real animals.

Think of it this way. An observation is like a photograph; an explanation is the caption you must justify. A caption can be wrong even when the photograph is accurate.

Worked example

“A solid remained on the filter” is an observation. “The sample contains an insoluble component” is an inference supported by that observation, assuming the filter and procedure worked properly.

Read the source: 2.2: Pure Substances · 2.7: Mixture · 2.10: Separating Mixtures · 2.14: Chemical Change

02 · Read a graduated cylinder and convert quantities using units that cancel.

A number needs a unit

In simpler language

Units are part of the measurement. Read carefully, then convert by multiplying by a ratio equal to one.

A measurement combines a numerical value with a unit. The SI base units include the meter for length, kilogram for mass, second for time, kelvin for temperature, and mole for amount of substance. Chemistry also commonly uses grams, liters, and milliliters. One liter is 1,000 mL; 1 mL occupies the same volume as 1 cm³. Prefixes describe powers of ten: kilo means 10³, centi means 10⁻², and milli means 10⁻³.

Read a clear, water-like liquid at the bottom of its curved meniscus, with your eye level with the liquid. Looking from above or below introduces parallax. On an analog scale, record the marked digits plus one estimated digit. With 1 mL divisions, a reading such as 26.4 mL is appropriate. A digital instrument is different: record its displayed resolution instead of inventing another digit.

Dimensional analysis uses equivalent quantities arranged as a fraction. Position the old unit so it cancels. The conversion changes the numerical representation, not the physical amount. Check the direction: a quantity expressed in smaller units usually has a larger number.

Think of it this way. Changing centimeters to meters resembles exchanging coins for bills: the number of pieces changes while the total value stays the same.

Worked example

Convert 350 mL to liters (three significant figures intended):

350 mL × (1 L / 1,000 mL) = 0.350 L

The mL units cancel. A second conversion is:

2.0 lbf·s × (4.45 N·s / 1 lbf·s) ≈ 8.9 N·s

Simply relabeling 2.0 would change the meaning.

03 · Count significant figures and round measurements and calculated results appropriately.

Report the precision you actually have

In simpler language

Do not report more detail than your tools can support.

Significant figures communicate the resolution of a measurement. Nonzero digits count; zeros between them count; leading zeros do not. Trailing zeros after a decimal point count. Thus 0.00450 has three significant figures, while 20.04 has four. A whole number such as 1500 can be ambiguous; 1.50 × 10³ explicitly has three significant figures. Exact counts and defined conversions do not limit precision.

For multiplication or division, round the final result to the fewest significant figures among measured inputs. For addition or subtraction, round to the least precise decimal place. Keep extra digits during intermediate calculations and round once at the end. Digits on a calculator are not additional experimental evidence.

Think of it this way. An enlarged photograph contains more pixels on your screen, but enlargement does not recover detail that the camera never captured.

Worked example

Addition is limited by the decimal place:

12.11 mL + 0.3 mL = 12.41 mL → 12.4 mL

Division is limited by significant figures:

2.4 g ÷ 1.12 mL = 2.142857… g/mL → 2.1 g/mL

The mass has two significant figures.

04 · Calculate density from displacement and compare it with reference data.

Use density to support an identification

In simpler language

Divide mass by the space the sample occupies. Replacing part of a well-mixed solution also removes that fraction of its dissolved material.

Density is mass per unit volume. A fully submerged, nonporous object displaces its own volume if it neither dissolves nor reacts:

ρ = m/V
V = Vafter − Vbefore

Read both menisci consistently. Bubbles, incomplete submersion, and water loss introduce error. Density is intensive: halving a uniform sample halves mass and volume, preserving their ratio. A reference-table match supports identification but cannot prove identity, compatibility, or toxicity. Use the mission’s supplied material information.

For the water-change case, replace a fraction f of well-mixed water. With equal final volume and no other sources or reactions:

Cfinal = Cinitial(1 − f) + Creplacementf

If replacement water contains none of the tracked substance, its contribution is zero.

Think of it this way. People per classroom illustrates a ratio: doubling both people and space leaves crowding unchanged. This does not describe atomic packing in detail.

Worked example

A 54.0 g object raises water from 20.0 to 40.0 mL:

V = 40.0 − 20.0 = 20.0 mL
ρ = 54.0 g / 20.0 mL = 2.70 g/mL

Separately, replace 25% of a 1.20 mg/L solution with substance-free water:

Cfinal = 1.20 × 0.75 = 0.900 mg/L

05 · Evaluate repeated measurements using their mean, spread, percent error, and possible bias.

Accuracy, precision, and an evidence-based decision

In simpler language

Measurements should cluster together and agree with a trustworthy reference.

Accuracy is agreement with a reference; precision is agreement among repeated measurements. A balance adding 0.20 g every time introduces systematic bias. Variable handling or reading errors produce scatter. Repeating trials estimates scatter but does not automatically remove bias.

Calculate the mean and compare it with a nonzero accepted reference:

Mean = sum of readings / number of readings
Percent error = |measured − accepted|/|accepted| × 100%

Retain units until they cancel. To justify a decision, state a claim, cite measurements, and explain their chemical relevance. Include uncertainty when it could change the decision.

Think of it this way. A clock that is always seven minutes fast is consistent but inaccurate. Averaging its readings will not repair its setting.

Worked example

For 9.8, 10.0, and 10.2 mL:

Mean = (9.8 + 10.0 + 10.2)/3 = 10.0 mL

Compare with a 10.5 mL reference:

Percent error = |10.0 − 10.5|/10.5 × 100% ≈ 4.8%

The mean alone does not describe the 0.4 mL spread.

Honors Can these metals really be distinguished?

Honors extension · builds on these expectations

TEKS C.2(B) analyze data by identifying significant statistical features, patterns, sources of error, and limitations;

TEKS C.2(C) use mathematical calculations to assess quantitative relationships in data; and

For small measurement bounds, the mission uses a first-order worst-case estimate:

δρ/ρ ≈ δm/m + δV/V

Add the two displacement reading bounds for δV. This is not a combination of independent statistical standard deviations. For 54.0 ± 0.1 g and 20.0 ± 0.2 mL, relative uncertainty is about 1.19%. Exact endpoints under those bounds are:

ρmin = 53.9/20.2 ≈ 2.668 g/mL
ρmax = 54.1/19.8 ≈ 2.732 g/mL

Divide minimum mass by maximum volume for the lower endpoint; reverse them for the upper endpoint.

Challenge: Why can a larger sample help distinguish two similar densities?

Show the reasoning

If absolute tool uncertainty stays similar, larger mass and displacement reduce relative uncertainty. If candidate density intervals still overlap, report that the identification is not resolved.

Read the source: 3.14: Measurement Uncertainty · Propagation of Uncertainty

Honors How much do repeated readings vary?

Honors extension · builds on these expectations

TEKS C.2(B) analyze data by identifying significant statistical features, patterns, sources of error, and limitations;

For at least two readings, sample standard deviation is:

s = √[Σ(xᵢ − x̄)²/(n − 1)]

Subtract the mean, square the differences, sum, divide by n − 1, then take the square root. The result has the same units as the readings. For 9.8, 10.0, and 10.2 mL:

s = 0.20 mL

Challenge: Can a small standard deviation prove accuracy?

Show the reasoning

No. It describes scatter, not bias. A calibration error can shift every result together. Compare the mean with a reference as well.

Keep the language precise

Glossary

Matter
Anything with mass and volume.
Substance
Matter with a defined composition, such as an element or compound.
Mixture
Substances combined in variable proportions.
Meniscus
The curved surface of a liquid.
Significant figures
Digits that express the precision of a measured quantity.
Density
Mass divided by volume.
Accuracy
Agreement with an accepted reference.
Precision
Agreement among repeated measurements.
Systematic error
A consistent bias caused by an instrument or method.
Uncertainty
A quantified or described limit on confidence in a measurement.
Mean
The sum of values divided by their count.
Standard deviation
A measure of the spread of a dataset.
